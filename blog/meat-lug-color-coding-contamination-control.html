Meat Lug Color-Coding for Contamination Control: A Protein Plant Playbook

Meatlugs Editorial • October 2026

In a multi-species or multi-zone protein operation, the humble meat lug does more than move product — it is a frontline control point for cross-contamination. A disciplined color-coding program turns an undifferentiated gray fleet into a visual management system that line workers, QA auditors, and sanitation crews can read at a glance. This guide walks procurement and food-safety teams through designing a color-coding scheme that holds up to USDA scrutiny and daily wash-down abuse.

Why Color-Coding Beats Labeling Alone

Adhesive labels peel in hot caustic wash cycles, and stenciled markings fade. Molded-in color, by contrast, survives the life of the lug. When the plastic itself carries the zone identity, there is no label to fall into product and no ambiguity during a shift change. For HACCP programs, documented color segregation provides clean evidence that raw and ready-to-eat streams never share the same container.

Building Your Zone Map

Start by mapping every point where product changes hands or risk category. A typical beef and poultry facility segments into raw receiving, grinding, marination, cooked/RTE, allergen handling, and inedible/rework. Assign one color per zone and resist the temptation to add more than six or seven — crews must memorize the scheme without a cheat sheet.

  • Red: raw beef and pork
  • Yellow: raw poultry
  • Green: cooked and ready-to-eat
  • Blue: allergen-containing product
  • White: edible rework and trim
  • Black: inedible and condemned

Durable, molded-in colors are available across most standard tub sizes, so you can match an existing fleet footprint without redesigning your racking or dollies. Specifying the Material

Color-coding only works if the base material tolerates your wash chemistry and temperature swings. Specify FDA-compliant polypropylene that resists caustic and acid sanitizers, holds dimensional stability from freezer to steam clean, and carries UV-stable pigment so colors do not drift over years of service. Smooth radiused interiors with no square corners eliminate the pockets where bacteria and trim collect. When evaluating a broader fleet beyond lugs, review the full line of PP food processing containers to keep resin spec and color standards consistent across totes, tubs, and transfer bins.

Rolling It Out Without Chaos

A phased rollout prevents the transition period from becoming its own contamination risk. Replace one zone at a time, beginning with the highest-risk interface — usually the raw-to-cooked boundary. Pull all legacy gray lugs from that zone in a single changeover so no mixed-color ambiguity lingers. Document the swap date for your audit trail, and update your sanitation SOPs so crews wash and stage lugs by color rather than by physical location.

Training and Verification

Post the color map at every hand-wash station and transfer point. During onboarding, quiz new hires on the scheme before they touch product. QA should spot-check during shifts for stray lugs — a yellow poultry lug staged in the cooked zone is an immediate corrective-action trigger. Over time, the visual discipline becomes reflexive, and auditors consistently flag well-run color programs as a plus during inspections.

The Payoff

A color-coded lug fleet is one of the lowest-cost, highest-visibility contamination controls a plant can deploy. It reduces the chance of a catastrophic raw-to-RTE mix-up, speeds sanitation staging, and gives auditors immediate confidence in your segregation discipline. Pair molded-in color with a clear zone map and phased rollout, and you convert a routine container purchase into a measurable food-safety upgrade.
