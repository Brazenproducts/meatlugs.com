How to Size a Meat Lug Fleet: Throughput, Buffer, and Replacement Math

Meatlugs Editorial • October 2026

Buy too few meat lugs and your line starves while crews wait on the wash loop; buy too many and you tie up capital and warehouse space in idle plastic. Right-sizing a reusable lug fleet is a logistics calculation, not a guess. This guide gives procurement and operations teams a repeatable framework for determining exactly how many lugs to own — and how many to reorder each year.

Start With Peak Throughput, Not Average

The anchor number is your peak-shift lug demand, not your annual average. Calculate how many lugs flow through your busiest zone during the busiest shift: product volume divided by fill weight per lug, rounded up. Averages hide the Friday surge or the pre-holiday run that actually determines whether you run short. Size to peak and the shoulder days take care of themselves.

The Four Pools Every Fleet Needs

A working fleet is never all on the line at once. Model it as four pools that must all be funded:

  • In-service: lugs actively holding product on the floor at peak
  • Wash loop: lugs cycling through sanitation, staging, and drying
  • In-transit float: lugs riding with shipments or sitting at a co-pack or DC awaiting return
  • Safety buffer: spare capacity for surges, damage, and quarantine holds

A common starting ratio is 1.0 in-service, 0.4 wash loop, 0.3 transit float, and 0.2 buffer — roughly 1.9x your peak in-service count. Closed-loop plants with on-site wash trend lower; operations that ship lugs off-site trend higher because the transit float balloons.

Wash-Cycle Timing Drives the Loop Pool

The size of your wash-loop pool is a direct function of cycle time. If a lug takes four hours to travel from empty return through wash, sanitize, dry, and re-stage, you need enough lugs in that pipeline to cover four hours of line demand. Faster wash throughput shrinks the pool and the total fleet. This is why nestable, smooth-walled designs pay off — they drain and dry faster and move through a tunnel washer cleanly. Replacement and Attrition Planning

Even durable PP lugs leave the fleet — damage, loss at customer sites, and end-of-life retirement. Track your annual attrition rate and build a standing reorder into the budget rather than scrambling when counts drop. A well-run plant on quality lugs often sees single-digit annual attrition, but off-site shipping and rough dock handling push it higher. Audit your fleet count quarterly so replacement orders stay ahead of shortfalls, and consider standardizing on a single supplier so replacements drop straight into existing racking and dollies. Facilities running mixed container types can consolidate attrition tracking by sourcing lugs alongside the rest of their PP food processing containers from one factory.

Capital vs. Cost-Per-Trip

Fleet sizing is where reusable economics prove out. Each lug is a one-time capital purchase amortized over hundreds or thousands of trips, versus the recurring spend of single-use packaging. Model the fully loaded cost per trip — purchase price divided by expected lifetime cycles, plus wash cost — and the right fleet size becomes a clear ROI decision rather than a budget argument. Larger fleets raise upfront capital but lower per-trip cost by reducing emergency top-up buys at premium prices.

Putting It Together

Size to peak, fund all four pools, tune the wash loop to cycle time, and budget for attrition up front. Do that and your line never starves, your capital never sits idle, and your cost-per-trip stays low — the full payoff of a reusable meat lug program.
